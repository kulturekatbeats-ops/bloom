How to time contractions

Timing contractions sounds technical when you're in the middle of it. It's actually two simple numbers — and a timer that does the math for you helps a lot.

The two numbers that matter

  • Frequency — start of one contraction to the start of the next. This is how far apart they are.
  • Duration — start to end of a single contraction. This is how long each lasts.

Record several in a row. In true labor, contractions tend to get closer together, last longer, and feel stronger over time.

The 5-1-1 rule

A widely used guideline for a first-time, low-risk labor: call your provider or head in when contractions are roughly 5 minutes apart, lasting 1 minute each, for at least 1 hour. Your provider may give you different numbers based on your situation, how far you live from the hospital, or a high-risk pregnancy — their instructions always come first.

True labor vs Braxton Hicks

  • Braxton Hicks (practice): irregular, don't get closer together, often ease with rest, water, or a change of position.
  • True labor: regular and getting closer, longer, and stronger; doesn't stop when you move around.
Go in sooner — regardless of the 5-1-1 timing — if your waters break, you have bleeding, you notice reduced baby movements, or you're preterm with regular contractions. When unsure, call your maternity unit. General information, not medical advice.

Common questions

What is the 5-1-1 rule for contractions?
Contractions about 5 minutes apart, lasting 1 minute, for at least 1 hour — a common signal to call for a first-time, low-risk labor. Follow your provider's numbers.
How do I tell real labor from Braxton Hicks?
Braxton Hicks are irregular and ease with rest. True labor gets regular, closer, longer, and stronger, and doesn't stop when you move.
When should I go to the hospital?
Per your provider — generally for waters breaking, bleeding, reduced movement, preterm contractions, or the 5-1-1 pattern.
