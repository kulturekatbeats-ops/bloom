Tracking a high-risk pregnancy

A high-risk label can be frightening. In practice it usually means closer monitoring and a few extra things to keep an eye on — and good organization makes that far less stressful.

What "high-risk" actually means

Being high-risk doesn't mean something will go wrong. It means your care team wants to watch more closely. A pregnancy may be considered high-risk because of:

  • Maternal age (under 17 or over 35)
  • Pre-existing conditions — high blood pressure, type 1 or 2 diabetes, thyroid or autoimmune conditions, heart or kidney disease
  • Pregnancy-related conditions — gestational diabetes, pre-eclampsia, placenta previa
  • Carrying twins or more
  • A history of pregnancy complications, preterm birth, or loss

Your provider decides this based on your individual history, and the plan is tailored to you. The single most useful thing you can do is keep accurate records and show up to appointments informed.

What to track between appointments

Follow your care team's specific instructions first. Commonly, high-risk pregnancies involve monitoring some combination of:

  • Blood pressure — if you've been asked to take readings at home, log them with the time of day so trends are visible.
  • Weight — sudden changes can matter; steady logging gives your provider a clearer picture.
  • Blood sugar — for gestational or pre-existing diabetes, alongside your glucose plan.
  • Baby's movements — once advised, usually around 28 weeks. How to count kicks →
  • Symptoms — headaches, swelling, vision changes, contractions, or anything specific to your condition.
  • Appointments & questions — a running list so a stressful visit doesn't erase what you meant to ask.

Warning signs to know

Your provider will give you a personalized list — that one always wins. Generally, contact your care team promptly for:

  • Severe or persistent headache, or changes in vision
  • Sudden swelling of the face or hands
  • Severe abdominal or upper-right belly pain
  • Vaginal bleeding or fluid leaking
  • Signs of preterm labor (regular contractions before 37 weeks) — how to time them
  • A noticeable decrease in your baby's usual movements
When in doubt, call. Maternity teams would far rather hear from you about a false alarm than have you wait. This page is general information, not medical advice.

Common questions

What makes a pregnancy high-risk?
Age, pre-existing conditions, pregnancy-related conditions like pre-eclampsia or gestational diabetes, carrying multiples, or a history of complications or loss. Your provider decides based on your situation.
What should I track in a high-risk pregnancy?
Whatever your team asks — often blood pressure, weight, blood sugar, baby's movements, and specific symptoms — plus appointments and questions in one place.
When should I call my provider?
For any warning sign they listed, and generally for severe headache, vision changes, sudden swelling, severe pain, bleeding, preterm contractions, or reduced movement. When in doubt, call.
